I'll look at how those example repos were built first, then map that approach onto a 人力 pipeline.

Yes, this is feasible, and I'd argue 人力 suits this approach better than video does. A human-made VOCALOID track is already a structured object: a list of notes, a library of samples, and an edit decision for each note. Melodyne and FL Studio are GUIs over that object, and code can hold it directly. Your description of the workflow is right. The piece it leaves out is timing metadata for each sample, meaning where in the clip the vowel starts. That turns out to matter about as much as the MIDI.

**What those videos actually do**

I looked at the two open repos. In the group-theory promo, the visuals are drawn frame by frame with Canvas, WebGL and JavaScript, and the soundtrack is synthesized in Python. A Node script then uses Chrome and FFmpeg to export the film, or to dump selected frames so they can be checked. The world.execute(me) PV works the same way: each frame is computed purely from the song's timestamp. The chat window is HTML built from dsh's own CSS and screenshotted with Playwright, and the TUI panel is drawn with PIL.

I'd push back on two parts of your framing:

- **"Learns how humans create."** I don't think that's quite what's happening. The model writes a deterministic program that drives existing tools: a browser engine, real CSS, ffmpeg. The elegance is that the result is editable source code rather than pixels, so fixing one thing means re-running the script, not regenerating the whole video.
- **"Pure."** That's a little generous for one of them. The README says the published cut used AI-generated character art and also included AI-generated video for the dancer. The open-source rebuild swaps in a stand-in.

**The part that's genuinely harder for audio**

Look at the frame-check option in the promo repo. The model renders frame 150, looks at it, and fixes the code. That closed loop is a big reason these videos are good. I can't listen. I can measure things like per-note pitch error against the MIDI, onset drift and clipping. I can also render a pitch track or spectrogram as an image and read that, which catches most mechanical mistakes. But whether it sounds like Otto actually singing, and whether it's funny, has to be judged by your ear. So the realistic design is that code does the first pass, and you are the listening loop.

**Pipeline**

The closest prior art is Otto 活字印刷. otto-hzys converts Chinese characters and letters to pinyin, finds each syllable in a voice library, and splices them together. That's speech with no pitch control. Your project is 活字印刷 plus a score. I didn't find an open singing version in a quick search, though Bilibili creators may well have private scripts.

1. **Stems.** Separate vocals from the instrumental with a RoFormer model (via UVR or python-audio-separator). If an official off-vocal version exists, use that instead.
2. **Melody → MIDI.** This is the weakest automatic step. SOME (OpenVPI's singing-oriented MIDI extractor) or basic-pitch will give you a draft. Expect octave errors, long melismas split into the wrong notes, and missed grace notes. If a fan transcription exists (扒谱, a UST, an SVP project), it beats any model. Either way, plan on correcting it in a piano roll.
3. **Sample library.** Run speech recognition plus forced alignment (WhisperX or Montreal Forced Aligner) over your clips. This slices them into syllables and tags each one with its pinyin, vowel onset time, median pitch and duration.
4. **Assignment.** This is the genuinely "intelligent" step: choosing which clip goes on which note. Each candidate gets a score based on:
   - how many semitones it has to be shifted (less is cleaner)
   - how much it has to be stretched
   - how close it sounds to the original lyric (空耳)
   - a penalty for overusing the same clip
   - placement, such as putting the signature catchphrase on the hook

   An LLM is good at the semantic and comedic judgments and at writing this scoring function. Ordinary code does the arithmetic.
5. **Render.** Do the pitch and time edits with WORLD (pyworld), Rubber Band (pyrubberband or Spotify's pedalboard), or PSOLA (parselmouth).
6. **Mix.** Mix in pedalboard, then assemble with ffmpeg.

The central artifact is a score file, the audio counterpart of that repo's `cues.json`. One note might look like this:

```json
{"t": 12.375, "dur": 0.25, "midi": 69, "lyric": "そ",
 "clip": "otto_0412.wav", "span": [3.214, 3.508], "vowel_at": 3.301,
 "alpha": 0.4, "keep_formant": true}
```

Because this timeline already says which clip plays when, the MAD video track (cutting to the source footage on every note) comes almost for free.

**What separates "authentic" from SoVITS-smooth**

SoVITS does timbre transfer: it produces a new, smooth performance in his voice. 人力's charm is collage. You recognise the exact utterance from that exact stream, rough edges included. Keeping the real samples is the audio equivalent of rendering with real CSS. Within that approach, three details do most of the work:

- **Align the vowel, not the clip start.** In a sung "sa", the *a* lands on the beat and the *s* starts early. At 120 BPM a 16th note is 125 ms, and an *s* can easily run 100 ms. If you line up the clip's start with the note's start, it drags by almost a full 16th. UTAU formalised this offset as "preutterance" in its oto.ini file. In effect, 人力 is building an improvised UTAU voicebank from found audio, and OpenUtau could even serve as the renderer.
- **Stretch only the vowel.** Fitting a 0.2 s "sa" into a 1 s note should give "saaaaa", not "sssssaaaaa".
- **Correct pitch only partially. This is the main control.** Take a note with MIDI number *m*, and a clip whose pitch curve is f₀(t) with centre f_c. The output pitch is:

  f_out(t) = 440 · 2^((m − 69)/12) · (f₀(t) / f_c)^α

  The first part is the note's frequency; the second part is the clip's own wobble, scaled by α. At α = 0, the result is flattened to a perfectly straight line on the note. At α = 1, his whole natural intonation moves up or down intact. Somewhere around 0.3–0.6 it is clearly a melody but still clearly his speaking voice. Setting α note by note is probably most of what people mean by "manual feel".

  Formant preservation is a similar control. Shift him up an octave without it and he becomes a chipmunk. Sometimes that's exactly the joke, so it should be a choice per note, not a global setting.

**Computer use vs Python**

Python, clearly. Having an AI click through Melodyne or FL for hundreds of notes is slow and fragile. What Melodyne actually contributes (note detection, formant-preserving pitch edits) has the open equivalents listed above. If you want a GUI for the final polish, have the script export a project file rather than operate the program. OpenUtau's .ustx and REAPER's .rpp are both plain text and easy to generate. FL's .flp is a binary format that's awkward to write.

**The song side needs no separation model**

The video you found isn't just narration-removed. It is split into three parts of equal length: music with the male humming, music without the humming, and a vocals-only track. Each part does a different job:

- **Part 2 (music, no humming)** is your backing track, as it is.
- **The guide melody** comes from Part 3, if Part 3 turns out to be the humming on its own. If Part 3 is the dialogue instead, you get the humming by subtraction: Part 1 = music + humming and Part 2 = music, so Part 1 − Part 2 = humming. That only works if the two are lined up to the sample at identical volume. We find the offset with cross-correlation, which slides one track against the other and picks the lag where they match best; it's one line in scipy.
- **Why subtraction will be slightly imperfect.** Bilibili encoded each part separately, so the compression noise won't cancel, and you'll get the humming plus a faint wash of music.
- **Why that doesn't matter here.** The guide is never heard in the final mix. It only gets pitch-tracked, so it needs to be trackable, not clean.

The humming is also a male voice, which helps: Otto's laughs won't need to travel as far in pitch.

**The separation model belongs on the Otto clip**

That video is a 2018 League of Legends stream recording, so game sound effects and background music are almost certainly under the laugh. If you pitch-shift the laugh, you pitch-shift that noise with it, and it smears audibly. Running a vocal separation model on just those 6 seconds fixes that.

**The real design problem: legato humming vs staccato laughter**

Humming is long and connected; laughing is a train of short "ha"s, roughly five per second. For each long note there are three options:

- **Laugh train.** Fill the note with "ha-ha-ha", every "ha" pitched to the note. This is the most 鬼畜 option.
- **Stretched single "ha".** Stretch one vowel to cover the note. It sounds more like singing, but risks a smeared, synth-like sound.
- **Hybrid.** A short train whose last "ha" is stretched to the note's end.

There is also a nicer option at the phrase level. Your source already has structure: laugh 1 (6 "ha"s), an inhale, laugh 2 (4), laugh 3 (3). If a humming phrase has around six notes, map laugh 1 onto it in its natural order, and put the inhale in the rest before the next phrase. A real laugh falls in pitch and loudness as it goes, and keeping that arc while it traces the melody is probably the funniest version and the most "human-made". It only works where the phrase lengths roughly fit, so the code should do this where it fits and fall back elsewhere.

**Three things to measure before writing the renderer**

These are the audio equivalent of checking frames before rendering the whole video:

1. **How voiced the laughs are.** A breathy "ha" carries pitch weakly. If it's mostly breath, the melody won't be recognisable no matter how well it's tuned. This is measurable as the fraction of frames where the pitch tracker finds a pitch. If it comes out low, hard tuning (α near 0) and giving the most voiced "ha"s to the important notes are the fallbacks.
2. **How far the pitch has to move.** Laughter usually sits well above speaking pitch, while male humming may sit low. Pulling a laugh down 8 or more semitones tends to get buzzy. There is an easy lever, though: transpose the whole melody up an octave. The melody stays the same tune and won't clash with the backing.
3. **Where the segment sits on the original video.** The stems run 3:19 and the official PV 3:22, so the stems are trimmed. Cross-correlating the music finds the offset.

**Reverberation: yes, and the plan handles it in two layers.**
